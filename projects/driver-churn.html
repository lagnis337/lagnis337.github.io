Driver churn prediction

Namma Yatri · Central data team · 2024–2025 · Owner, end to end

Industry project. Code and data are proprietary; this is a methods write-up using only publicly stated figures.

Problem

Namma Yatri is a zero-commission ride-hailing platform: drivers pay a subscription rather than a per-ride cut, so driver retention is both the supply side of the marketplace and a direct revenue line. Around 42% of active drivers were churning, where churn meant no completed rides for 30 days.

Retention effort — ops calls and in-app coin incentives — was being spent broadly, or after a driver had already gone quiet. The goal was to identify drivers likely to churn early enough for an intervention to matter, and to give ops and customer success a ranked list of who to reach each day.

Data

One row per driver. Features were rebuilt from platform activity every day and the model scored roughly 150,000 active drivers daily.

Label. Whether the driver went 30 days without a completed ride after the scoring date. The label is only known a month later, so the training set always lagged production by 30 days and every live prediction was unverified for that long — a constraint that shaped how impact was measured (below).

36 features, in five families:

  • Supply behaviour — hours online, days active, day vs. night rides
  • Demand funnel — ride requests received → accepted → completed; cancellation rate
  • Earnings — average fare, average trip distance, earnings per active hour
  • Feedback — rider ratings and complaint signals
  • Context — city and geo-zone, tenure on the platform, and change in activity relative to the driver's own recent baseline

Approach

Model. An ensemble of a gradient-boosted tree model and a random forest, with the two scores averaged. Neither model alone was consistently better across months; the average was more stable, which mattered for a list that ops teams acted on every day — a model whose top-ranked drivers changed sharply from one day to the next would have been hard to work with.

Output. A churn-risk score per driver, produced daily and handed to product and customer-success teams as a ranked list.

Action layer. Flagged drivers were routed to two interventions: a call from the ops team to understand why they were dissatisfied, and coin incentives in the app. The calls turned out to be as valuable as the model — see below.

Churn was tracked as monthly cohorts — for each month's active drivers, what share had churned at M0, M1, M2, M3 — rather than a single rolling rate, so the effect could be read cohort by cohort as the system ran. Over three months in production the monthly churn rate fell from roughly 42% to 30%.

The number that doesn't fit in a table: the ops calls became a real-time channel for driver feedback. Reasons drivers gave for leaving fed directly into changes to pricing, the driver–rider matching algorithm, and new product features. The model's job was to decide who to call; the calls then improved the product the model was trying to retain drivers on.

What I'd do differently

The before/after cohort comparison shows the system worked, but it can't separate the model's contribution from the interventions' — some flagged drivers would have stayed regardless of a call, and some left despite one. A held-out group of at-risk drivers who were deliberately not contacted would have measured the true lift, and reframing the problem as uplift modelling (who changes behaviour when contacted) rather than risk scoring would have targeted incentive spend better. I'd also replace the 30-day binary label with a time-to-churn (survival) formulation, which uses the information in when a driver stops rather than just whether they crossed a threshold.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. The modelling stack was Python (pandas, scikit-learn, gradient boosting) with daily batch scoring.
