Driver churn prediction

Every morning, rank 150,000 drivers by how likely they are to stop driving, and hand ops a list they can act on.

Namma Yatri · Central data team · 2024–2025 · Owner, end to end

Industry project. Code and data are proprietary; this is a methods write-up.

XGB + RFXGBoost and random forest, averaged & calibrated, 36 features
AUC ~0.80ranks a driver who will churn above one who won't about 80% of the time (held-out month)

Problem

Namma Yatri is a zero-commission ride-hailing platform: drivers pay a subscription, not a per-ride cut, so driver retention is both marketplace supply and a direct revenue line. Around 42% of active drivers were churning — a non-contractual setting, with no cancellation event: churn is inferred from inactivity (no completed rides for 30 days).

Before

No systematic targeting. Ops looked at drivers who hadn't been active for more than a week and reached out — by which point many had already left.

Goal

Score every driver daily on their probability of churning in the next 30 days, and give ops and customer success a ranked list sized to what they can act on.

Data

Unit
Driver-day; ~150,000 active drivers scored each morning. Drivers below a minimum tenure excluded from training.
Label
churn_30d = 1 if no completed rides in the 30 days after the scoring date. Known only a month later, so the latest month is never labelled and impact is measured on cohorts, not on the model's own predictions.
Features
36, most over weekly and monthly windows, all computed strictly before the scoring date. Structured on the RFM framework (recency, frequency, monetary value) plus feedback and context.
Leakage
The recency features — days since last trip, time since last active — encode the label if computed after the scoring date. Every window is pinned to it.
FamilyFeatures
RecencyDays since last completed ride; recent activity vs. the driver's own longer-run baseline (a busy driver slowing down ≠ a quiet driver staying quiet)
FrequencyRides completed, days active, hours online; demand funnel — requests → accepted → completed, cancellation rate; share of night rides
MonetaryAverage fare, average trip distance, earnings per online hour; subscription status (days left on plan)
FeedbackRider ratings and complaints
ContextCity and geo-zone; tenure; zone-level demand (requests per online driver — is there work here?); contacted by ops recently

Approach

  1. Two models, one score

    XGBoost (gradient-boosted decision trees) — the stronger model alone; captures interactions such as high online hours and falling acceptance rate. Random forest (scikit-learn) — weaker alone, but its errors are less correlated with the boosted model's. Equal-weight average of the two probabilities, then calibrated so the score reads as a probability. The average was more stable month to month than either model — which matters more than a marginally higher AUC for a list ops act on daily.

  2. Split by time, never at random

    Train on earlier months, validate on a later held-out month. Churn drifts with incentive schemes and seasonality, and a random split leaks the same driver's rows across train and test. Retrained monthly on a rolling window as each month's labels matured.

  3. Decision rule sized to capacity

    Top of the list — around 100 drivers a day — gets an ops call; the next band gets an in-app coin incentive. Cut-offs set by what teams can act on, not by a probability threshold. The nightly Airflow job (a scheduled pipeline) writes the list to a shared sheet that product and customer success work from.

  4. Close the loop

    Ops tag the reasons drivers give on calls; the categories go to product and, as a contacted recently feature, back into the model.

The 42% → 30% figure is a before/after comparison on the whole driver base, so it includes everything else that changed in the quarter — including the product change below.

Key insight Drivers working evenings wanted their last rides to end near home, and were declining trips or logging off rather than head the wrong way. That became "Go Home" mode — switch it on and you receive requests whose drop-off is near your house. Other tagged reasons fed changes to pricing and driver–rider matching. The model decided who to call; the calls changed the product; the product changed churn.

What I'd do differently

A holdout group

Deliberately not contacting a random slice of high-risk drivers would separate the call's effect from selection and from concurrent changes like Go Home.

Uplift, not risk

Uplift modelling — predicting who changes behaviour when contacted rather than who is at risk — so the ~100 daily calls go to drivers a call can actually keep.

Time-to-churn

A survival-analysis formulation (modelling when an event happens) uses when a driver stops, not just whether they crossed a 30-day threshold.

Drift monitoring

Weekly checks on the top features, so an incentive change shows up in the model's metrics the week it happens.

Related work

Framing adapted from Tran (2021) — RFM-based, non-contractual churn prediction for riders on a public Uber dataset of ~1,400 users (ICABDE 2021). This project moved it to the supply side — drivers, whose churn removes capacity — at ~150,000 drivers scored daily in production, with an action loop attached.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. Stack: SQL feature build orchestrated nightly with Apache Airflow; Python with pandas, XGBoost and scikit-learn; scores delivered daily to a shared sheet.
