Driver churn prediction

Every morning the model ranks about 150,000 drivers by the risk that they stop driving, and the operations team works down the list.

Namma Yatri · Central data team · 2024–2025 · Owner, end to end

Industry project. The code and data are proprietary, so this page describes the method only.

~150kdrivers scored every morning
XGB + RFXGBoost and random forest, averaged & calibrated, 36 features
AUC ~0.80ranks a driver who will churn above one who won't about 80% of the time (held-out month)
42% → 30%monthly churn, three months in production

Problem

Namma Yatri charges no commission. Drivers pay a subscription rather than a cut of each fare, which makes retention both the marketplace's supply and a direct revenue line. About 42% of active drivers were churning. Nobody cancels a subscription in an obvious way here, so there is no event to label: a driver counts as churned after 30 days with no completed ride.

Before

Nothing was targeted. Ops pulled up drivers with no activity for a week and called them, by which point many had already gone.

Goal

Score every driver daily on the probability they stop within 30 days, then hand ops and customer success a ranked list cut to the length they can actually work through.

Data

Unit
One row per driver per day. About 150,000 active drivers are scored each morning. Drivers with very little history on the platform are kept out of training.
Label
churn_30d = 1 when a driver completes no ride in the 30 days after the scoring date. The label only arrives a month later, so the most recent month is never labelled and impact is measured on cohorts rather than on the model's own predictions.
Features
36 features, most over weekly and monthly windows, all computed strictly before the scoring date. The frame is RFM (recency, frequency, monetary value), with feedback and context on top.
Leakage
The recency features, days since the last trip and time since the last activity, encode the label if you compute them after the scoring date. Every window stops at that date.
FamilyFeatures
RecencyDays since the last completed ride, and recent activity against the driver's own usual level. A busy driver slowing down is not the same as a quiet driver staying quiet.
FrequencyRides completed, days active, hours online. The demand funnel of requests, accepted requests and completed rides, plus cancellation rate and the share of night rides.
MonetaryAverage fare, average trip distance, earnings per online hour, and subscription status with days left on the plan.
FeedbackRider ratings and complaints
ContextCity and geo-zone, time on the platform, and demand in the zone measured as requests per online driver, since a driver needs work nearby. Plus a flag for a recent call from ops.

Approach

  1. Two models, one score

    XGBoost (gradient-boosted decision trees) is the stronger of the two on its own. It picks up combinations such as long hours online alongside a falling acceptance rate. The random forest (scikit-learn) is weaker, but it makes different mistakes.

    The score is an equal-weight average of the two probabilities, calibrated so it reads as a real probability. The average held steadier month to month than either model alone, and for a list ops works every day that stability matters more than a marginally better AUC.

  2. Split by time, never at random

    Train on earlier months, validate on a later held-out month. Churn drifts with incentive schemes and with the season, and a random split would scatter the same driver's rows across train and test. Retraining ran monthly on a rolling window, as each month's labels matured.

  3. Decision rule sized to capacity

    The top of the list, about 100 drivers a day, gets a call from ops. The next band gets in-app coins. Those cut-offs come from what the teams can handle, not from a probability threshold. A nightly Airflow job writes the list to a shared sheet that product and customer success work from.

  4. Close the loop

    Ops tag the reason each driver gives on the call. The categories go to product, and a recent-call flag goes back into the model.

A randomised holdout measured the calls: about 20% of high-risk drivers were held back and received none. The called group churned less than that holdout, which is the one part of the result the calls can be credited with. The 42% to 30% figure is a wider measurement. It compares two periods across all drivers and carries the product changes described below, since those reached the holdout too.

Key insight Drivers who work in the evening want the last ride of the day to end near their home. They refused rides in the other direction, or they went offline. This caused the Go Home function. When a driver starts this function, the driver receives only the requests with a destination near the home. Other reasons from the calls caused changes to the prices and to the match between a driver and a rider. The model selected the drivers to call, the calls changed the product, and the product decreased the churn.

What I'd do differently

Measure the product changes

The holdout isolates the calls, but the product changes those calls prompted reached every driver, the holdout included. A staggered release by city would measure Go Home and the pricing changes on their own.

Uplift, not risk

Uplift modelling predicts who changes behaviour when contacted rather than who is at risk, so the hundred daily calls would go to the drivers a call can actually keep.

Time-to-churn

A survival model predicts when an event happens, using the moment a driver stops rather than only whether they crossed a 30-day line.

Drift monitoring

Check the top features weekly, so a change to an incentive scheme shows up in the model's metrics the week it lands.

Related work

The framing comes from Tran (2021), which applies RFM to non-contractual churn among riders on a public Uber dataset of about 1,400 users (ICABDE 2021). This project moves the same frame to the supply side, where a driver leaving removes capacity, and runs it in production over about 150,000 drivers a day with an action loop attached.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. Stack: a SQL feature build orchestrated nightly by Apache Airflow, Python with pandas, XGBoost and scikit-learn, and a daily delivery of scores to a shared sheet.
