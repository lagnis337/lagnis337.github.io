Driver churn prediction

Namma Yatri · Central data team · 2024–2025 · Owner, end to end

Industry project. Code and data are proprietary; this is a methods write-up using only publicly stated figures.

Problem

Namma Yatri is a zero-commission ride-hailing platform: drivers pay a subscription rather than a per-ride cut, so driver retention is both the supply side of the marketplace and a direct revenue line. Around 42% of active drivers were churning. This is a non-contractual churn setting — there is no cancellation event, so churn has to be inferred from inactivity; here it meant no completed rides for 30 days.

There was no systematic targeting: ops would look at drivers who hadn't been active for more than a week and reach out — by which point many had already left. The goal was to identify drivers likely to churn early enough for an intervention to matter, and to give ops and customer success a ranked list of who to reach each day.

Data

Unit of prediction: driver-day. Roughly 150,000 active drivers were scored every morning. Drivers below a minimum tenure were excluded from training, since their activity windows are mostly empty.

Label. churn_30d = 1 if the driver completed no rides in the 30 days after the scoring date. The label is only known a month later, so the most recent month of history is never labelled and every live prediction is unverified for 30 days — which is why impact was measured on monthly cohorts rather than on the model's own predictions.

36 features, most over both weekly and monthly windows, each computed strictly from activity before the scoring date. The recency features — days since last completed trip, time since last active — are the ones that leak if you're careless: computed as of any moment after the scoring date they encode the label directly, so the feature build pins every window to the scoring date. Structured on the RFM framework from the churn literature — recency, frequency, monetary — plus two families it doesn't cover:

FamilyFeatures
RecencyDays since last completed ride; recent activity relative to the driver's own longer-run baseline (a trend feature — a busy driver slowing down looks different from a quiet driver staying quiet)
FrequencyRides completed, days active, hours online; the demand funnel — requests received → accepted → completed, cancellation rate; share of night rides
MonetaryAverage fare, average trip distance, earnings per online hour; subscription status (days left on the current plan)
FeedbackRider ratings and complaints
ContextCity and geo-zone; tenure; zone-level demand — requests per online driver in the driver's area, a proxy for whether there is work to be had; whether the driver was contacted by ops recently

Approach

Model. Two models, trained on the same features, with their predicted probabilities averaged with equal weights:

  • XGBoost — gradient-boosted trees, the stronger of the two on its own; captures interactions such as high online hours combined with a falling acceptance rate, which is a different kind of at-risk driver from one whose hours are simply dropping.
  • Random forest (scikit-learn) — slightly weaker alone, but its errors are less correlated with the boosted model's, so the average was more stable month to month than either model. For a list that ops act on every day, a stable ranking matters more than a marginally higher AUC.

The averaged score was calibrated so it could be read as a probability, not just a rank.

Training protocol. Split by time, never at random: train on earlier months, validate on a later held-out month. Churn drifts with incentive schemes and seasonality, and a random split leaks the same driver's rows across train and test. Retrained monthly on a rolling window as each new month's labels matured.

Decision rule. Two tiers sized by capacity, not by a probability threshold: the top of the list — around 100 drivers a day — got a call from the ops team; the next band got an in-app coin incentive. The list was written to a shared sheet by the nightly DAG, which is where product and customer success worked from.

Churn was tracked as monthly cohorts — for each month's active drivers, the share churned at M0, M1, M2, M3 — so the effect could be read cohort by cohort as the system ran. Over three months in production the monthly churn rate fell from roughly 42% to 30%. This is a before/after comparison on the whole base; it includes everything else that changed in the period (see below).

What drove the predictions. The most important features were utilisation (online hours and how much of that time produced rides) and completed-trip metrics — how many rides, and how that compared with the driver's own recent baseline.

What the calls said. Reasons drivers gave were tagged by the ops team into categories, and the categories went to product. The clearest example: drivers working evenings wanted their last rides to end near home, and were declining or logging off rather than accept a trip in the wrong direction. That became a "Go Home" mode — a driver switches it on and receives requests whose drop-off is near their home. Other tagged reasons fed changes to pricing and to driver–rider matching. The model decided who to call; the calls changed the product.

What I'd do differently

The cohort comparison shows the system worked but can't separate the model's contribution from the interventions' or from concurrent product changes like Go Home. A holdout — a random slice of high-risk drivers deliberately not contacted — would have measured the true lift of a call, and reframing the problem as uplift modelling (who changes behaviour when contacted) rather than risk scoring would have targeted the ~100 daily calls better. I'd also replace the 30-day binary label with a time-to-churn (survival) formulation, which uses the information in when a driver stops rather than just whether they crossed a threshold, and add drift monitoring on the top features so a change in incentives shows up in the model's metrics the week it happens.

Related work

The framing was adapted from Tran (2021), who applied RFM-based, non-contractual churn prediction to riders on a public Uber dataset of ~1,400 users (published as "Rider Churn Prediction Model for Ride-Hailing Service", ICABDE 2021). This project moved the idea to the supply side — drivers, whose churn removes capacity from the marketplace — at ~150,000 drivers scored daily in production, with an action loop attached to the predictions.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. Stack: SQL feature build orchestrated nightly with Airflow; Python with pandas, XGBoost and scikit-learn; scores delivered daily to a shared sheet.
