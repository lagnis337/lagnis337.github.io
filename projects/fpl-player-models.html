Problem

Every week, 6M+ FPL managers pick a squad and score the points their players earn on the pitch. I've finished in the top 12% by building ad-hoc player models each week — in past seasons on Fantasy Football Scout's member data. This is the disciplined version of that workflow, on public data, so the results are reproducible and the evaluation is honest.

Question

How many points will each player score in the next gameweek — and does a learned model pick a better XI than simple rules?

Constraint

Every feature must be computable before the deadline. The backtest must use models the way a manager would: retrain on what's happened, predict the coming week, repeat.

Data

Unit
Player-fixture: one row per player per match, 2022-23 to now (114,202 rows), from the vaastav archive of the official FPL API. Earlier seasons lack xG/xA and are excluded.
Target
FPL points scored in that fixture.
Features
79, all pre-deadline. Rolling form over the last 3 / 6 / 10 fixtures and season-to-date (points, minutes, starts, goals, assists, bonus, xG, xA, ICT…), shifted by one fixture so a player's own result never leaks into its features; team and opponent strength; home/away and rest days; price, ownership, transfers; position; availability at the deadline.
Availability
The FPL API only exposes injury status for the current week, so historical status was reconstructed from the archive's git history: for each gameweek, the last weekly snapshot committed before its first kickoff. 152 gameweeks recovered; players flagged unavailable average 0.001 points.

Approach

  1. Walk-forward, not cross-validation

    For each of the 75 gameweeks in 2024-25 and 2025-26: train on every fixture that had kicked off before the gameweek started, predict it, move on. Random splits would leak the same player's later form into earlier predictions.

  2. Baselines first

    Last-3-fixture mean and season-to-date mean — what a casual manager does by eye.

  3. Three learned models

    Ridge regression; gradient-boosted trees (300 rounds, 31 leaves, ≥100 samples per leaf, L2-regularised); and a hurdle model — a classifier for will they play? times a regressor for points if they do, because most of the variance in FPL points is whether the player is on the pitch at all.

  4. Score what a manager cares about

    Per-fixture error (MAE, RMSE, rank correlation) and the points captured by picking each method's best legal XI (1-4-4-2) every week — against an oracle that picks with hindsight, so the gap to the ceiling is visible.

  5. Check the data before believing the result

    Two probes — one for missing values, one for leakage — that turned out to matter more than the modelling (below).

Learned models beat the naive baselines by ~5 points per gameweek on picks (~10%) and ~7% on error, consistently across both seasons. The three learned models are within noise of each other — the features are the ceiling, not the algorithm.

Finding 1 — the archive's expected-points column is contaminated

FPL publishes its own expected points per player, stored in the archive as xP. It's the obvious benchmark, and an early version used it as a baseline and as a stacking feature — and captured 87% of the oracle. Too good. Two checks showed why:

  • Missing stored as zero. For 27 of 38 gameweeks in 2025-26 the column is 0 for nearly everyone, including starters who scored — weeks the scraper didn't capture, not predictions of zero.
  • Post-match information. Among players who started, archived xP correlates 0.50 with actual points versus 0.15 for the best genuine pre-match feature, and within a player's season it sits +1.3 above their average on exactly the matches where they scored. Consistent with the field being re-scraped after the gameweek, when FPL's "form" already included that week's points.

So xP was excluded from every result above; the code paths are kept so the finding is reproducible.

Finding 2 — knowing who's injured didn't help

Availability looked like the single most valuable missing signal (unavailable players score ~0). The ablation says otherwise: MAE 0.997 → 0.989, rank correlation 0.714 → 0.725, XI points 54.0 → 52.2 — inside week-to-week noise. The snapshots average ten days old, and the players any method picks are regular starters whose availability the form features already encode. The experiment that could change this needs deadline-time snapshots, which don't exist historically — so the pipeline now needs to collect them going forward.

Key insight Two of the three biggest results in this project were wrong the first time, and both were caught by checking the data rather than the model. The stacked-on-xP model that captured 87% of hindsight was a leak; the availability feature that "had to" help didn't. The honest number — ~10% over naive picks — is smaller and real.

What I'd do differently

Snapshot at the deadline

A scheduled job capturing the FPL API at every deadline gives hours-old availability instead of days-old. This is the ablation I couldn't run.

A clean external benchmark

Bookmaker odds or a third-party expected-points source captured before each deadline, to replace the contaminated xP as the bar to beat.

Optimise the squad, not the XI

Budget, transfers and captaincy under FPL's real constraints — closing the loop with how I actually play.

Position-specific models

Goalkeepers and forwards earn points differently; one model with position one-hots may be leaving signal on the table.
