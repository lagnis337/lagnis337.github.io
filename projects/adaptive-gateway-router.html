Adaptive payment-gateway router

Razorpay · Global Payments · 2026 · Owner: framing, simulator, agent, benchmark, shadow-mode and rollout measurement; platform engineering owned production integration

Industry project at a payments company. Code and data are proprietary; this is a methods write-up. Production volumes, traffic shares, gateway identities, incident details and revenue figures are deliberately omitted; every number shown is from the synthetic benchmark.

Problem

A payments platform routes each transaction to one of several gateways. Gateways degrade suddenly, partially and temporarily, and static routing rules keep sending traffic to a degraded gateway until someone notices and re-weights by hand. Detection is measured in hours, and every hour of mis-routed traffic is failed payments and lost revenue. Incidents of this shape motivated the project.

Rejected: predict

Forecast each gateway's success rate and route to the best forecast. Fails exactly when it matters: gateway failures are non-stationary, rare in training data, and by the time a forecast updates the damage is done.

Chosen: respond

An agent that observes only the outcomes of transactions it routes — success, failure, latency — and shifts traffic away from a gateway the moment its observed health drops. No forecasting; the skill being learned is adaptation.

Key insight Gateway failures are close to unpredictable, but reacting to them fast is tractable. Framed as a non-stationary multi-armed bandit (a sequential decision problem where each choice reveals only its own outcome, and the best choice changes over time) — explore enough to notice a gateway has recovered, exploit enough not to waste transactions on one that hasn't — the problem stops needing a model of the future.

Observations, actions, environment

Constraint
The router never sees a gateway's true state. Everything it knows is inferred from transactions it sent — the same constraint production has.
Action
Choose one of N gateways for each transaction.
Observation
8 features per gateway (a 40-vector for five gateways), each from a rolling window of that gateway's recent outcomes; neutral defaults until a gateway has 5 observations.
Reward
The signal the agent optimises: +1.0 success / −0.5 failure; −0.0005 × latency_ms; +0.2 for switching away from a gateway whose success-rate trend has dropped more than 5 points — rewarding proactive switching before failures accumulate.

Simulator. Each gateway carries hidden parameters — base success rate, volatility, health, and a degradation pattern from {stable, slow-decay, sudden-crash, bursty}. A turbulence manager starts a degradation with 0.02% probability per transaction, drops success rate 2 points per step to a floor of 5–10%, holds for 100–500 transactions, then recovers at 1 point per step; latency spikes of 2–5× occur independently. Gradual degradation and slow recovery were deliberate: sudden crashes are easy to detect, slow decay looks like noise. The environment is more turbulent than production, so a router that copes here has margin.

Production features. The same 8 observations are computed live from rolling windows of recent outcomes, per gateway, and served to the router.

Approach

  1. Score every gateway with an upper confidence bound (UCB)

    UCB = empirical success rate + exploration bonus + staleness bonus − latency penalty. The exploration bonus is the UCB1 algorithm's √(log N / nᵢ) scaled by 0.15 — large for gateways sampled rarely, shrinking as evidence accumulates. Staleness adds +0.2 to any gateway not sampled in the last 100 transactions, so a recovered gateway gets re-tried. Latency above 100 ms is penalised.

  2. Detect drift and explore harder

    Before each decision, compare the current gateway's success over its last 5 transactions with the 5 before. A drop of more than 10 points flags degradation and raises the exploration rate to 10% to find alternatives. Otherwise exploration decays exponentially from 8% to a 2% floor (τ = 2,000 transactions).

  3. Never do the obviously wrong thing

    A hard floor: no traffic to a gateway whose observed success rate is below 20%. A general safety check, cheap in the benchmark, and the kind of guarantee a payments team needs before trusting a learning system.

  4. Learn online, not offline

    No retraining step. The tracker is the model; every transaction updates it. Gateway behaviour changes faster than any offline model could be refreshed.

  5. A PPO policy, trained but not in the loop

    A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent — a standard deep reinforcement-learning algorithm — with a small two-layer neural network (64×64 multilayer perceptron) was pre-trained in a Gymnasium environment on the simulator with the reward above was pre-trained on the simulator as a candidate prior for the online layer. The benchmarked and deployed router makes its decisions from the observation tracker alone; the PPO policy is not in the decision path. It's kept as the natural next experiment (see below).

Benchmark

Two routers under the same observation-only constraint, in the turbulence simulator, at three scales. The rule-based router stands in for conventional threshold-based routing: start on a random gateway, switch when observed success rate over the last 5 drops below 50%, 5-transaction cooldown, re-evaluate every 50.

10 million transactions ran in 20.5 minutes (~8,100 transactions per second). Average latency: 208 ms rule-based vs. 233 ms adaptive — ~25 ms paid for switching and probing.

Why the gap is so large. The rule-based router switched 84 times in ten million transactions. Gradual degradation never trips its 50% threshold until most of the damage is done, and with no exploration it never discovers that a gateway has recovered — so it sits on degraded gateways for long stretches. The adaptive router's 1.55M switches are not churn; they are the mechanism that keeps it on whichever gateway is healthiest right now.

Why scale matters. At 100K transactions the rule-based router looked fine — 269 degradations aren't enough to expose it. The failure only becomes visible at 1M+. A small benchmark would have shipped the wrong conclusion.

What this benchmark doesn't show. Only two routers were compared. A greedy baseline, a frozen-PPO variant and an oracle that routes to the true-best gateway would separate "reacting at all" from "reacting well" and show the ceiling — that's the first thing I'd add (below).

Deployment

Shadow mode
One week alongside production, logging the recommended gateway per transaction without executing it.
Rollout
Then live traffic, behind guardrails that roll back automatically if success rate or latency regresses against control. Success rate on the routed traffic was higher than the control.
Measurement
I owned the measurement. A production figure isn't reported here; the benchmark above was the basis for the rollout decision.

What I'd do differently

Complete the ablation

Random, greedy, frozen PPO and an oracle alongside the two routers benchmarked. The interesting number is adaptive vs. oracle — what exploration costs.

Put the PPO prior to the test

Wire the trained policy into the online layer and measure whether it beats the tracker-only bandit. If it doesn't, drop it.

Use the literature's bandits

Staleness probes and drift boosts are heuristics doing the job of sliding-window or discounted UCB, or Thompson sampling with forgetting — the standard algorithms for bandits whose arms change over time. Try those as the online layer.

Route per segment

Gateways degrade per payment method or bank, not globally. A contextual bandit over gateway × segment is the obvious next model.

Replay real incidents

The simulator was built by the same person as the router. Replaying logged production incidents would be a more convincing test than a synthetic environment.

Report regret over time

Cumulative regret against the oracle shows when the router loses — at onsets or during recoveries — which is where the next improvement is.

Related work

The UCB1 algorithm (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer, 2002) for the exploration bonus; discounted and sliding-window UCB (Garivier & Moulines, 2011) as the principled treatment of non-stationary arms this router approximates with heuristics; PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) for the pre-trained policy.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Razorpay.
