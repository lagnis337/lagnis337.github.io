Driver safety enforcement

Read every rider complaint the same day, in any language, and match the consequence to the severity. Minor cases are handled automatically; a person decides the serious ones.

Namma Yatri · Central data team · 2024–2025 · Owner: classifier, scoring and action logic

Industry project. The code and data are proprietary, so this page describes the method only.

Problem

Riders leave feedback after every ride, a rating with an optional comment, and raise support tickets in the app or through the call centre. Most of it concerns fares, routes and cancellations. A small share describes something unsafe: dangerous driving, harassment, alcohol, threats. On a platform whose main promise is that a rider gets home safely, that small share carries most of the weight.

Before

Support agents read tickets one at a time and escalated the alarming ones. Nobody could see a driver's full history, so a pattern of complaints looked like a series of unrelated events, and serious cases surfaced days late.

Goal

Read all the feedback the same day, rank the safety complaints by severity, and give the driver a consequence that fits and that is the same for everyone. Minor cases automatic, serious ones decided by a person.

The text was the hard part. Feedback arrived in Kannada, Hindi and English, and often in Kannada or Hindi typed in the Latin alphabet, where spelling varies from rider to rider. Keyword rules and single-language models both fall over on that.

Data

Unit
One piece of feedback, either a rating comment or a support ticket, tied to a ride, a driver and a rider.
Languages
Kannada, Hindi, English and romanised mixtures. The Sarvam AI translation API, built for Indian languages, converts each item to English before classification, so the classifier only ever learns English patterns.
Labels
Five severity groups plus a bucket for everything that is not a safety complaint. The support team labelled the first set against a written guide, and after launch every case ops reviewed became a new labelled example.
Imbalance
Safety items are a small share of all feedback and the severest groups are rarer still, which drives the modelling and threshold choices below.

Approach

  1. Translate first, then classify

    The Sarvam translation API turns every comment into English, including the romanised Kannada and Hindi that defeat most models. That moves the multilingual problem out of the model, which leaves a simple, inspectable classifier as enough.

    The classifier runs TF-IDF (term frequency, inverse document frequency) over word and character n-grams, short runs of words or letters that survive the spelling noise translation leaves behind, and a linear model assigns the group. Inspectable mattered: ops could see which words drove a flag.

  2. Threshold for recall at the top

    The two mistakes cost very different amounts. A missed assault report is far worse than one more case in a review queue. Thresholds on the severe groups are set for recall, catching as many real cases as possible and accepting false alarms, which the review step then filters out.

  3. Severity chooses the lever, the window scales with it

    Flags roll up per driver and per group. Groups 1 to 3 lead to a block whose length scales with severity: permanent at the top, a fixed number of days lower down.

    Groups 4 and 5 lead to a lower priority. The driver stays online but drops down the ride-matching order for a window, and is told why. Losing a week of rides is the warning. Matching already supported priority tiers, but this was the first time anyone leaned on them hard, and it turned enforcement into a graded cost rather than an on-off switch.

  4. Humans on every block

    Lower priority applies automatically. Every block goes to a queue first, showing the original text, its translation, the driver's history and the recommended action. Driver-ops confirm or overturn with one click, an overturned case returns as a corrected label, and drivers can appeal through driver support.

  5. Daily batch, not real time

    Same-day review is what the problem needed. Streaming would have added complexity without changing outcomes.

Complaints, tagged rides and rating are all before-and-after comparisons, so they carry everything else that changed in the period. And most drivers with one complaint never get a second under any policy, so part of that result would have happened anyway. The 12,000 figure counts drivers who went through the lighter lever; it is not a measured change in behaviour.

Key insight Most drivers with a complaint are not dangerous. They had a bad day, or they had a habit that nobody told them about. A system with only a warning and a block is too weak for these cases, or too strong. The priority in the ride matching gives a third option. The cost agrees with the problem, the driver feels it immediately in the earnings, and good behaviour removes it. Most of the approximately 12,000 drivers in this group got no new complaint, and a person examined the evidence before each of the approximately 550 blocks.

What went wrong, and what changed

Safety as a fare complaint

Some riders filed fare disputes under safety because the category gets attention, and a few used it to retaliate after an argument. The human step on blocks exists largely for that.

Vocabulary drift

A fare change produced a wave of complaints in phrasing the classifier had never seen. The fix is a weekly hand-checked sample of feedback the classifier did not flag, which catches misses rather than false alarms.

Early pushback

In the first weeks driver-ops pushed back on blocks they disagreed with. That produced the rule that no block lands without human confirmation, and that rule is what made the system trusted enough to keep.

What I'd do differently

Measure the lever

Randomise the length of the lower priority for group-5 drivers, so the data shows how much cost actually changes behaviour instead of the window being someone's judgement call.

Rider credibility

Use the rider's own complaint history as a feature. A first complaint from a frequent rider and a fifth from a rider in a fare dispute are not the same evidence.

Held-out evaluation

Build a fixed labelled set to track precision and recall per group over time, and record how often a person overturns a block in the queue. That overturn rate is a quality signal the system already produces daily.

Related work

Graded consequences are standard practice in marketplace trust-and-safety teams, and the Santa Clara Principles set the usual expectations for notice and appeal. Two things here are less common. The consequence runs through the matching service's own priority tiers, and a translation step takes the multilingual problem off the model, which keeps the classifier simple. The models from Sarvam AI are built for Indic languages, which is what made that translation reliable enough to build on.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. Stack: Python with scikit-learn (TF-IDF and a linear classifier) and pandas, the Sarvam AI translation API, SQL over ride and feedback data, a daily scheduled job, and the block queue and priority controls exposed by the matching service.
