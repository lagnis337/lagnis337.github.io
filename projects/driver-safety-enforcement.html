Driver safety enforcement

Read every rider complaint the same day, in any language, and apply a consequence that fits the severity — automatically for minor cases, with a person deciding the serious ones.

Industry project. Code and data are proprietary; this is a methods write-up.

Problem

Riders leave feedback after every ride — a rating with an optional comment — and raise support tickets in the app or through the call centre. Most of it is about fares, routes and cancellations. A small fraction describes something unsafe: rash driving, harassment, intoxication, threats. On a platform where rider safety is the product's main promise, that fraction matters far more than its size suggests.

Before

Support agents read tickets one at a time and escalated the alarming ones by judgement. Nobody had a view across a driver's history, so a pattern of complaints looked like a series of one-offs, and serious cases surfaced days late.

Goal

Read every piece of feedback the same day, sort the safety-relevant ones by severity, and apply a proportionate, consistent consequence to the driver — automatically for minor cases, with a human decision for serious ones.

The text was the hard part. Feedback arrived in Kannada, Hindi and English, and often in romanised Kannada or Hindi typed on an English keyboard — spellings vary from rider to rider, so keyword rules and single-language models both fail.

Data

Unit
One feedback item — a rating comment or a support ticket — attached to a ride, a driver and a rider.
Languages
Kannada, Hindi, English and romanised mixes. Every item was translated to English with Sarvam AI's translation API (built for Indian languages) before modelling, so the classifier only had to learn English complaint patterns.
Labels
Five severity buckets plus not a safety complaint. The first labelled set was hand-tagged by the support team against a written guide; after launch, every case reviewed by ops became a new labelled example.
Imbalance
Safety-relevant items are a small minority of all feedback, and the most severe buckets are rarer still — which set the modelling and threshold choices below.

Approach

  1. Translate first, then classify

    Sarvam's translation API turned every comment into English — including the romanised Kannada and Hindi that trip up most models. That moved the multilingual difficulty out of the model entirely, so a simple, inspectable classifier was enough: TF-IDF (term frequency–inverse document frequency) features over word and character n-grams (short runs of words or letters, which tolerate the spelling noise that survives translation), into a linear classifier. Inspectable mattered — ops could see which words drove a flag.

  2. Threshold for recall at the top

    The cost of the two mistakes is asymmetric: a missed assault report is far worse than an extra case for a human to review. Thresholds on the severe buckets were set for recall — catch as many genuine cases as possible, accepting some false alarms — and the resulting false positives were absorbed by the review step rather than tuned away.

  3. Severity chooses the lever, the window scales with it

    Each driver's flags roll up by bucket. Buckets 1–3 lead to a block whose duration scales with severity — permanent at the top, a fixed number of days lower down. Buckets 4–5 lead to deprioritisation: the driver stays online but drops down the ride-matching order for a window, and is told why. Losing ride volume for a week is the warning. Matching already supported priority tiers; this was the first time the mechanism was customised this heavily, and it turned enforcement into a graded cost rather than an on/off switch.

  4. Humans on every block

    Deprioritisation is automatic. Any block goes to a queue with the original text, its translation, the driver's history and the recommended action; driver-ops confirm or overturn with one click. Overturned cases go back as corrected labels. Drivers can appeal through driver support.

  5. Daily batch, not real time

    Feedback is reviewed the same day, which is what the problem needed; a streaming system would have added complexity without changing outcomes.

The complaint reduction is a before/after comparison, so it includes everything else that changed in the period. And "no further flag" after one deprioritisation is partly what any policy would see — most drivers with a single complaint never get a second — so ~12,000 counts drivers who went through the lighter lever, not a measured change in behaviour.

Key insight Most drivers who get a complaint are not dangerous; they had a bad day, or a habit nobody had called out. A binary warn-or-block system either lets that slide or over-punishes it. Making ride priority the lever gave enforcement a dial: a cost proportionate to the offence, felt immediately in earnings, reversible by behaving. Most of the ~12,000 drivers who went through it never appeared again; the ~550 who were removed had a person look at the evidence first.

What went wrong, and what changed

Safety as a fare complaint

Some riders filed fare disputes under safety because it gets attention, and a few used it to retaliate after an argument. The human step on blocks exists largely because of this.

Vocabulary drift

A fare change produced a wave of complaints in new phrasing that the classifier had never seen. Fix: a weekly hand-checked sample of unflagged feedback, to catch misses rather than false alarms.

Early pushback

Driver-ops objected to false blocks in the first weeks. The rule that no block applies without confirmation came out of that, and made the system trusted enough to keep.

What I'd do differently

Measure the lever

Randomly vary the deprioritisation window for bucket-5 drivers to learn how much cost actually changes behaviour, instead of picking a window by judgement.

Rider credibility

A rider's own complaint history as a feature — a first complaint from a frequent rider and a fifth from a rider with a fare dispute are not the same evidence.

Held-out evaluation

A fixed, hand-labelled evaluation set to report precision and recall per bucket over time, and the block queue's overturn rate tracked as a metric — the one quality signal the system already produces every day.

Related work

The graded-consequence idea is standard in trust-and-safety practice at marketplaces; the unusual part here is using the matching engine's own priority tiers as the consequence, and offloading the multilingual problem to translation so the classifier stays simple. Sarvam AI's models are built for Indic languages, which is what made the translation step reliable enough to build on.

Code & artifacts

Proprietary to Namma Yatri. Stack: Python with scikit-learn (TF-IDF, linear classifier) and pandas; Sarvam AI translation API; SQL over ride and feedback data; daily scheduled job; block queue and priority controls exposed by the platform's matching service.
